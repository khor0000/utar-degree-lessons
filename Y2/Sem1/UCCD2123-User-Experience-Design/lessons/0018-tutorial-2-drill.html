The eight questions

T2 Q1. What are the four main activities in the UX design process, and why is the process considered iterative? (Lesson 11)

reveal model answer

The four main activities are understanding (finding out what the system must do and how it fits with other things), design (conceptual and physical design), envisionment (making ideas visible, such as prototypes) and evaluation, which is central to delivering a good UX and happens at every step. The process is iterative, not linear: it can start at any activity, the activities can happen in any order, and the team goes round them many times, so both its understanding of needs and its design solutions evolve.

T2 Q2. Why is evaluation central to the UX design process, and at what stages does it occur? (Lesson 12)

reveal model answer

Evaluation is central because everything gets evaluated at every step of the process, so a design is checked against its criteria from the first idea to the finished product. It occurs at initial concepts (paper prototypes compared with competitor products), at design options (quick experiments comparing alternatives), at usability testing (formative, with users trying a prototype) and at the finished product (summative, against a standard such as ISO 9241, plus data analytics after deployment).

T2 Q3. What are some key methods used in the "Understanding" phase of UX design? (Lesson 13)

reveal model answer

Key understanding methods are interviews (structured, semi-structured or unstructured conversations about needs and problems), questionnaires (broad surveys across many people), probes (self-documentation over time), card sorting (how users naturally categorize information), working with groups (focus groups and workshops), fieldwork or observation of people in their real environment, and artefact collection, which studies the documents and forms people create and use. Designers combine asking with observing, because people find some activities hard to describe and may describe the official procedure instead of what they really do. Together these methods produce the requirements, the functions the system must perform and the qualities it must have.

T2 Q4. How is conceptual design different from physical design in UX? (Lesson 14)

reveal model answer

Conceptual design is abstract: it concerns the information content and functions a system needs to achieve its purpose, and what someone must know to use it, without deciding how they are distributed. Physical design makes it concrete: it structures interactions into logical sequences, allocates functions and knowledge between people and devices, and fixes the look and feel through colours, shapes and sizes. The two are not sharply separated, and designers move between them, but conceptual design answers what the system is for while physical design answers how it works and looks.

T2 Q5. What is the role of envisionment in UX design, and what techniques are commonly used? (Lesson 15)

reveal model answer

Envisionment is the activity of making design ideas visible, such as sketches, storyboards, mood boards, wireframes and lo-fi or hi-fi prototypes, so that the designer and the people who will use the system can think about the ideas, explore alternatives and evaluate them. It brings abstract ideas to life, exposes flaws and generates new ideas, and it is how the team communicates with colleagues and clients. Commonly used techniques are sketching, storyboarding, mood boards, maps, wireframes and prototypes, including Wizard of Oz prototypes in which a human plays the missing feature. The representation should suit its purpose, so a polished version is not always needed.

T2 Q6. What are some methods used in evaluating UX designs, and how do they differ? (Lesson 12)

reveal model answer

The main methods are expert-based (heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough), which are quick but can miss problems real users have; participant-based (user testing), which gives a realistic picture from real people; and data analytics (such as A/B testing), which uses data from live use. They differ in who does the checking and when.

T2 Q7. What are personas, and how do they aid in the UX design process? (Lesson 16)

reveal model answer

Personas are concrete representations of the different types of people a system is designed for, built from research data and PACT analysis (people, activities, contexts and technologies). They capture the goals and aspirations of users as well as their functional needs, so the team can see the design from another person's point of view. They aid the design process by keeping the team designing for real user needs rather than its own assumptions, and by showing where different people need different features. Several personas are needed because different people have different goals, and a persona is neither a real user nor an average.

T2 Q8. What is the difference between conceptual and concrete scenarios in UX design? (Lesson 17)

reveal model answer

A conceptual scenario is abstract: it describes what people do and what the system must offer, without fixing the technology or the interface, and it is used to generate ideas and requirements. A concrete scenario is made from a conceptual one by adding design decisions, such as a particular device or a particular way of showing the information, so it can be used to envision and evaluate one design. The difference is the level of abstraction, not the length, and the two are not a clean break, since the more specific a scenario is, the more concrete it becomes. A concrete scenario can then be formalized as a use case for programmers.
