The procedure

Use the same three steps for every question. Each step is one click, and the arrow keys move between them.

Step 1. Close the lesson, take a pen, and write the answer as far as you can. A blank point is information, so leave it blank rather than guessing.

Step 3. For each point you missed, go back to the lesson named under the question and reread only that part. Then write the answer again on paper.

Questions 5 to 8

T2 Q5. What is the role of envisionment in UX design, and what techniques are commonly used? (Lessons 15 to 15b)

reveal model answer

Envisionment is the activity of making design ideas visible, such as sketches, storyboards, mood boards, wireframes and lo-fi or hi-fi prototypes, so that the designer and the people who will use the system can think about the ideas, explore alternatives and evaluate them. It brings abstract ideas to life, exposes flaws and generates new ideas, and it is how the team communicates with colleagues and clients. Commonly used techniques are sketching, storyboarding, mood boards, maps, wireframes and prototypes, including Wizard of Oz prototypes in which a human plays the missing feature, and design workbooks, which keep the design's own proposals over a project. The representation should suit its purpose, so a polished version is not always needed.

T2 Q6. What are some methods used in evaluating UX designs, and how do they differ? (Lessons 12b and 12d)

reveal model answer

The main methods are expert-based (heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough), which are quick and cheap and need no users, but can miss problems real users have; participant-based (user testing), which gives a realistic picture from real people but is slower and costlier; and data analytics (such as A/B testing), which needs a live system and shows what people do, not why. They differ in who does the checking and when.

T2 Q7. What are personas, and how do they aid in the UX design process? (Lessons 16 to 16b)

reveal model answer

Personas are concrete representations of the different types of people a system is designed for, built from research data and PACT analysis (people, activities, contexts and technologies). They capture the goals and aspirations of users as well as their functional needs, so the team can see the design from another person's point of view. They aid the design process by keeping the team designing for real user needs rather than its own assumptions, and by showing where different people need different features. Personas are used in understanding, envisionment, design and evaluation; for example, an expert walks through a task as one persona. Several personas are needed because different people have different goals, and a persona is neither a real user nor an average.

T2 Q8. What is the difference between conceptual and concrete scenarios in UX design? (Lessons 17 to 17c)

reveal model answer

A conceptual scenario is abstract: it describes what people do and what the system must offer, without fixing the technology or the interface, and it is used to generate ideas and requirements. A concrete scenario is made from a conceptual one by adding design decisions, such as a particular device or a particular way of showing the information, so it can be used to envision and evaluate one design. The difference is the level of abstraction, not the length. A scenario is concrete on each point where it fixes the interface or device, or the split of functions between people and devices, and conceptual where it leaves those open (Benyon ch3, 3.5). A concrete scenario can then be formalized as a use case for programmers.
