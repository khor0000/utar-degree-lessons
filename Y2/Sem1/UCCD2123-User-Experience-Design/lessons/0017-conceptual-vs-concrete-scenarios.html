Conceptual and concrete scenarios

A conceptual scenario describes an activity without fixing the technology or the interface, so it can generate ideas. A concrete scenario adds specific design decisions, such as the device and how the screen works, so it can envision and evaluate one particular design.

Lesson 8a defined a scenario as a short story of a persona doing an activity in a context. Lesson 16 covered the persona half. This lesson covers the scenario half: the book describes scenarios as stories about people undertaking activities in contexts, using technologies (Benyon ch3, 3.4, PDF p88).

Looks like the one-line user story from Lesson 8a, but it differs. A user story is a single line written for developers. A scenario is a short story about a person doing an activity in a context. A conceptual scenario is still a story of people doing something, with the technology left out.

The scenario chain

The book describes four scenario types in a chain, linked by three processes: designers abstract from stories to conceptual scenarios, add design constraints to reach concrete scenarios, and then formalise the design ideas as use cases (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p92). The diagram follows one example, booking a doctor's appointment, through the chain. Step through it.

Stage 1, story. Stories are real-world experiences, with the small details a formal account would leave out. The book's example is a parent who rings the surgery for a child's earache (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p92 to p93).

Stage 2, conceptual scenario. The designer abstracts from the story. People can contact the surgery at any time, from any internet-connected device, and see the free times for each doctor (PDF p93). No device or interface is chosen yet.

Stage 3, concrete scenario. The designer adds decisions. Andy uses a PC at work, logs in, selects Dr Fox, and sees her diary for the next two weeks (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94). Now a particular interface and device are fixed.

Stage 4, use case. The concrete scenarios are formalised as a case of how the system is used, given to programmers. Each use case covers many slight variations in circumstances (PDF p94 to p95).

Which statement about conceptual and concrete scenarios is correct?

Right. The difference is abstraction: the concrete scenario fixes technology and interface choices, and the conceptual one leaves them open.
Not quite. The difference is abstraction, not length. Concrete scenarios name specific technology and interface decisions. Conceptual scenarios leave them out.

A conceptual scenario says that people can book a doctor's appointment from any device connected to the internet. Which change makes it more concrete?

Right. Naming a device and the way the diary is shown fixes design decisions, so the scenario becomes concrete.
Not quite. Concreteness comes from design decisions such as a device or an interface. Removing words, or adding more story detail, does not change the level of abstraction.
