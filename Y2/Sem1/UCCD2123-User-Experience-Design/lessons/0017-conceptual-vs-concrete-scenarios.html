Conceptual and concrete scenarios

A conceptual scenario describes an activity without fixing the technology or the interface, so it can generate ideas. A concrete scenario adds specific design decisions, such as which device is used and how the screen works, so it can be used to envision and evaluate one particular design. The difference is the level of abstraction, not the length.

Lesson 8a defined a scenario as "a short story of that persona doing an activity in a context". Lesson 16 showed the persona half of that pair. This lesson covers the scenario half. The book describes scenarios as "stories about people undertaking activities in contexts using technologies" (Benyon ch3, 3.4, PDF p88), and there are four types in a chain.

Why this matters. A scenario is how the team pictures what people do before any screen exists. Choosing the right level of detail is what lets a scenario be useful at each stage.

Focus on. The four types in the chain, and what separates the two middle types: the level of abstraction.

Looks like the one-line user story from Lesson 8a, but it differs. A user story is a single line written for developers. A scenario is a short story about a person doing an activity in a context. The word "conceptual" may also pull you towards a mental model, which is a different thing. A conceptual scenario is still a story of people doing something, with the technology left out (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p93).

The scenario chain

The book's Figure 3.11 shows four types, and three processes that connect them: "Designers abstract from the details of stories to arrive at conceptual scenarios. They specify design constraints on conceptual scenarios to arrive at concrete scenarios. Finally, they formalize the design ideas as use cases" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p92). The diagram follows one example, booking a doctor's appointment, through the chain. Step through it.

Stage 1, story. Stories are people's real-world experiences, with the small details a formal account would leave out. The book's example is a parent who rings the surgery for a child's earache, is offered a Tuesday slot that does not suit, and accepts a Thursday morning slot that makes the child late for school (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p92 to p93).

Stage 2, conceptual scenario. The designer abstracts from the story. The book's version: "People will be able to contact the doctors' surgery, at any time, using any device connected to the internet, and see the times that are free for each doctor" (PDF p93). A device or interface is not chosen yet.

Stage 3, concrete scenario. The designer adds decisions. Andy "uses a PC and the internet at work", logs in, selects Dr Fox, and "her diary is displayed for the next two weeks" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94). Now a particular interface and device are fixed.

Stage 4, use case. The concrete scenarios are formalized as "a case of how the system is used", given to programmers. "Each use case covers many slight variations in circumstances" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94 to p95).

Abstraction is the difference

Stage 2 and stage 3 describe the same appointment. The line between them is the level of abstraction. The book says the conceptual booking scenario "could be made more abstract by not specifying that the internet should be used or more concrete (that is, less abstract) by specifying that the booking should be made from a computer" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p93). Each added decision moves the scenario towards concrete. This is the same split as Lesson 14: conceptual design asks what the system is for, and a conceptual scenario keeps that question abstract.

Length is not the test. A long conceptual scenario that names no device is still conceptual, and a short scenario that names a PC and a two-week diary is concrete. The book also says "there is not a clean break between conceptual and concrete scenarios. The more specific the scenario is about some aspects, the more concrete it is" (PDF p94).

The lecture uses the same idea with its own example. A concrete scenario is "used for evaluation and design decisions" (Lecture 8, PDF p17), and the lecture's example of a banking app describes a busy professional who "needs to quickly check his account balance" (Lecture 8, PDF p18).

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q8 on paper: What is the difference between conceptual and concrete scenarios in UX design?

reveal model answer

A conceptual scenario is abstract: it describes what people do and what the system must offer, without fixing the technology or the interface, and it is used to generate ideas and requirements. A concrete scenario is made from a conceptual one by adding design decisions, such as a particular device or a particular way of showing the information, so it can be used to envision and evaluate one design. The difference is the level of abstraction, not the length, and the two are not a clean break, since the more specific a scenario is, the more concrete it becomes. A concrete scenario can then be formalized as a use case for programmers.
