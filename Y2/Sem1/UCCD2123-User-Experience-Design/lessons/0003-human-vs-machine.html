The question that tells them apart

Machine-centred design makes the person adapt to the machine. Human-centred design makes the machine fit the person.

The two differ in the designer's first question. The machine-centred designer asks "What can this machine do?" and then makes the person fit. The human-centred designer asks "What is the person trying to do?" and then makes the technology fit. An old ATM that asks for an account type code before a student who only wants RM50 can continue is machine-centred: the student must fit.

Machine-centred design is chosen because it is quicker for the designer, who skips studying people. The book says the cost moves to the user, because the view is quicker and easier for the designer but not for the person who ends up using the product (Benyon ch1, Box 1.1). It is not a job title either: many designers still take the machine-centred view. Programmers can be human-centred too.

UX is not only about keeping people happy. The book's aim is interactive systems, products and services that fit with people and the way they live (Benyon ch1, Aims), so fit, usability and enjoyment all count.

A flowchart for telling them apart

Take one example: entering your date of birth.

Ask one question: must the person memorise, type exactly or decode, or does the system handle that?

"Type 2004-05-17 exactly, or get an error." The person does the work, so this is machine-centred.

"Pick a date on a calendar." The system does the work, so this is human-centred.

Sort these

A login fails and the screen shows only "Error 401".
A login fails with "Wrong password. Try again or reset it."
To pay a stall, you type a 16-digit merchant code.
To pay a stall, you scan a QR code.
A long form times out and wipes your answers.
A long form saves your answers and warns you before timing out.

These examples match the book's design principles (Benyon ch5, 5.5). A wrong-password message is feedback (principle 7). A QR scan instead of typing 16 digits is a constraint (principle 9), because it stops the slip from happening. A biometric login after a forgotten PIN is recovery through another path (principle 8). Words like "fool-proof" point to a constraint, and "fallback" points to recovery.

T1 Q1: What is UXD, and how does it differ from traditional design? Write 3–4 sentences.

reveal model answer

Q1. UXD is developing high-quality interactive systems, products and services that are usable and enjoyable, covering the user's whole journey. It aims to meet user needs and be intuitive, accessible and positive to use. Traditional design tends to start from the product or machine, so people must adapt. UXD is human-centred: it starts from people's needs, goals and contexts.
