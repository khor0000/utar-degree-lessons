Lesson 11 · Tutorial 2 Q1

Four activities, one loop

The UX design process has four activities: understanding, design, envisionment and evaluation. Evaluation sits at the centre, and the process is iterative: the team goes round these activities many times, and each round refines the design.

A process is easy to picture as a line of steps, done once from top to bottom. This one is not that. The process "can start at any stage", and "activities can be performed in any order" (Lecture 2, PDF p4). The book says the same thing in its own words: it is "an iterative process, not a linear one" (Benyon ch3, 3.1, PDF p77). You may begin with an existing design, with a prototype, or with understanding, and then go round many times.

Why this matters. Every later step you learn (finding out what people need, drawing ideas, checking them with people) sits in one of these four activities. Knowing which activity you are in tells you what to do next.

Focus on. The four names, what each one produces, and why evaluation is at the centre.

Looks like Lesson 9's cycle (also a loop), but it differs. Lesson 9's loop is a change in the world: technology changes activities, and the changed activities set new requirements. This loop is the designer's own work on one system. Its four activities can be entered at any point, and evaluation is at the centre of all of them.

The loop

The book's Figure 3.2 (PDF p78) puts evaluation in the middle and the other three activities around it. Each arrow goes both ways, so any activity can feed evaluation, and evaluation can send you back to any activity. Step through one example, a campus food-ordering app, round the loop twice.

Round 1, understanding. Students queue at the canteen, and some have no cash. The activity produces a need and a requirement: order and pay from a phone in under a minute.

Round 1, design. The team shapes the requirement into a conceptual design (menu, order, pay, pick-up time) and a physical design (a large Pay button).

Round 1, envisionment. A clickable prototype of the order screens, so students can react to something real.

Round 1, evaluation. Three students try the prototype. Two miss the Pay button, and one cannot find how to go back. These findings feed the next round.

Round 2, design. The team moves Pay to the bottom of the screen and adds a Back arrow. The physical design changes; the need does not.

Round 2, envisionment. The prototype is rebuilt with the two fixes.

Round 2, evaluation. Five students try it, and all of them pay. They then ask when their food will be ready. That is a new need.

Round 2, back to understanding. The new need goes back to understanding, as an order-status requirement. The loop goes on, and the next round can start here.

What each activity is

  • Understanding: finding out what the system must do, what it has to be like, and how it fits with other things (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p78).
  • Design: creating ideas, then shaping them into a conceptual design (the abstract idea of the system) and a physical design (the concrete detail that makes it work) (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79).
  • Envisionment: making design ideas visible, such as a sketch or a prototype (a working mock-up), so the designer and the users can react to them (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p82).
  • Evaluation: checking the design with the people who will use it, at every step of the process (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p78 and p83).

Evaluation is at the centre because "any of the other design activities will be followed by an evaluation" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p83). Every activity on the ring hands its output to evaluation.

Slides and textbook disagree on "ideation". The slide defines it as evaluating and refining ideas (Lecture 2, PDF p5); the textbook uses it for the creative generation of ideas (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79). This lesson follows the textbook.

Two quick checks

Right. Evaluation runs throughout, and the activities can be entered in any order.

Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q1 on paper: What are the four main activities in the UX design process, and why is the process considered iterative?

reveal model answer

The four main activities are understanding (finding out what the system must do and how it fits with other things), design (conceptual and physical design), envisionment (making ideas visible, such as prototypes) and evaluation, which is central to delivering a good UX and happens at every step. The process is iterative, not linear: it can start at any activity, the activities can happen in any order, and the team goes round them many times, so both its understanding of needs and its design solutions evolve.
