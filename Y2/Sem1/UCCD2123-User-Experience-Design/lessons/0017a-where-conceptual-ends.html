Where conceptual ends: a test for one scenario

Conceptual and concrete are relative, and there is no clean line between them. A scenario can be checked on its own: it is concrete on each point where it fixes a device, an interface, or who does which part, and conceptual everywhere else.

Lesson 17 gave the difference as abstraction, not length. The next question is where the boundary falls, and how to tell for one scenario without comparing it with another.

Why this matters. A scenario is how the team pictures use before a screen exists. If you cannot tell which level a scenario is at, you cannot choose the right level for the job.

Looks like the Lesson 14 split (two boxes, one line between them), but for scenarios the book gives degrees, not a line. In Lesson 14, conceptual and physical design were two kinds of work. For scenarios, the book says "there is not a clean break between conceptual and concrete scenarios. The more specific the scenario is about some aspects, the more concrete it is" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94). The same idea appears in Chapter 9: "There are degrees of concreteness in scenarios" (Benyon ch9, 9.4, PDF p249).

Relative, not absolute

A scenario is concrete or conceptual about a particular aspect. One scenario can be concrete about the device and still conceptual about the goal, so the question is which aspect.

Where the line falls

There is no single line. The book names what moves a scenario across the middle: a concrete scenario "begin[s] to dictate a particular interface design and a particular allocation of functions between people and devices" (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94). So the two markers are the interface or device, and the split of functions. Both can be checked by reading the scenario.

The ladder: one scenario, three versions

Here is one canteen story, written three ways. Step through it.

Rung 1, the story. A real experience with the small details kept: the queue, the time, the hunger in class. Nothing has been designed yet.

Rung 2, conceptual. The details are stripped away. The scenario names what students do and what they get back, and it names no device, screen or control. It would fit a phone app, a web page or a counter kiosk.

Rung 3, concrete. Design decisions are added. The phone is the device, the Order button is the control, and the phone shows the code while the stall scans it. The split of functions between person and device is now fixed.

The test, for one scenario

Working rule (the book gives the two markers in words, not as a checklist): read the scenario one sentence at a time and ask two questions.

  1. Does the sentence name a device, a screen or a control, such as a phone, a page, a button or a search bar? Then it fixes the interface or the technology.
  2. Does the sentence say who does which part, such as "the phone shows" or "the counter scans"? Then it fixes how functions are split between people and devices.

If neither question is yes, the sentence is conceptual, however long it is. If either is yes, that sentence is concrete on that point. A scenario can pass for some sentences and fail for others, and the book's "degrees" are exactly that.

A borderline case, settled by the test

Take this sentence: "Students get a message on their phone when the order is ready." It looks conceptual, because it is short and plain. Apply the test. "A message when ready" describes the outcome, which the conceptual rung already allows, so the notification itself is conceptual. "On their phone" names a device, which the first question catches. The sentence is therefore concrete on the device and conceptual on the outcome.

You might reasonably wonder whether any named control makes a scenario concrete. Under this test, yes: "Students are told when the order is ready" is conceptual, while "Students tap the Ready button" is concrete.

Slides and book differ on one example. Lecture 8 (PDF p3) files "consider filtering options" under conceptual design. The book's wording would treat a named filter control as concrete, so this lesson follows the book. The slide's wording fits the function "narrow the results", which is conceptual.

Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson. Answer T2 Q8 on paper: is the difference between conceptual and concrete scenarios only a matter of length? Say no, state the difference, then take one sentence of your own canteen scenario and apply the two questions to it.

reveal model answer

No. The difference is level of abstraction. A conceptual scenario describes what people do and get, without naming a device, control or split of functions. A concrete scenario adds those decisions, so it fixes the interface and who does which part. The line is not clean: a scenario can be concrete about some aspects and conceptual about others. For example, "Students order a canteen meal from their phone" names a device, so it is concrete on that point, while "Students order a canteen meal in advance" is conceptual. Apply the two questions to each sentence: does it name a device, screen or control, and does it say who does which part?
