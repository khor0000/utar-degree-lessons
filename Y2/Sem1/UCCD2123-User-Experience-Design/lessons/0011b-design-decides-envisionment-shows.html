Design decides, envisionment shows

Physical design decides how the system will work and look. Envisionment makes any design idea visible, such as a sketch, so that it can be explored and evaluated. A sketch can show a physical design decision, which is why the two overlap.

Lesson 11a covered understanding. This lesson covers the second and third activities, design and envisionment, which are easy to confuse because a sketch belongs to both.

Looks like the design step itself, but it differs. Design makes the decision. Envisionment makes the decision visible, so people can react to it.

Design: ideas first, then two kinds of design

Design starts with idea generation, the creative step (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79). It then decides in two parts. Conceptual design asks what the system is for. Physical design decides how it will work and look (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79 and p81). Lesson 14 covers the difference between the two.

Envisionment: making a decision visible

Envisionment: making design ideas visible, such as a sketch, storyboard or prototype, so that the team can think about them, explore them and evaluate them (Benyon ch8, Aims, PDF p211). Benyon ch8, PDF p211 to p213

The two overlap in one place. Take the Pay button on the canteen app. Deciding that Pay sits at the bottom of the screen is a physical design decision. Drawing that screen on paper is envisionment, because the drawing makes the decision visible. The same sketch does both jobs.

The decision. The team decides where Pay goes. This is physical design, because it says how the screen works and looks.

The sketch. The decision is drawn on paper. This is envisionment, because it makes the decision visible. The sketch is the same object, seen as physical design and as envisionment.

The reaction. People look at the sketch and react. Only now can the team evaluate the decision.

Why a decided design still needs envisionment

You might reasonably wonder why envisionment is needed once the look is decided. A decision on paper is hard for other people to react to. The book's reason is that designs need to be visualised, both to help designers clarify their ideas and to let people evaluate them (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p82). Envisionment does that job, whether the decided item is physical or conceptual.
