Where conceptual ends: a test for one scenario

A scenario can be checked on its own. It is concrete on each point where it fixes a device, an interface or who does which part, and conceptual everywhere else.

Lesson 17a said the difference is abstraction, not length. This lesson gives a test that works on one scenario, without comparing it with another.

Looks like a checklist at the canteen counter: does the order name a stall, a machine or a person? If it does, that part of the order is concrete.

What moves a scenario across the middle

The book names what makes a scenario concrete: it begins to dictate a particular interface design and a particular allocation of functions between people and devices (Benyon ch3, 3.5, PDF p94). So the two markers are the interface or device, and the split of functions. Both can be checked by reading the scenario.

One canteen story, three versions

Here is one story written at three levels. Step through it.

Rung 1, the story. A real experience with the small details kept: the queue, the time, the hunger in class. Nothing has been designed yet.

Rung 2, conceptual. The details are stripped away. The scenario names what students do and what they get back, and it names no device, screen or control. It would fit a phone app, a web page or a counter kiosk.

Rung 3, concrete. Design decisions are added. The phone is the device, the Order button is the control, and the phone shows the code while the stall scans it. The split of functions between person and device is now fixed.

The test, for one scenario

Read the scenario one sentence at a time, and ask two questions.

  1. Does the sentence name a device, a screen or a control, such as a phone, a page, a button or a search bar? Then it fixes the interface or the technology.
  2. Does the sentence say who does which part, such as "the phone shows" or "the counter scans"? Then it fixes how functions are split between people and devices.

If neither question is yes, the sentence is conceptual, however long it is. If either is yes, that sentence is concrete on that point. A scenario can pass for some sentences and fail for others.

Which sentence is concrete on the interface, not just on the device?

Right. It names a control, a button, and a screen, so it fixes the interface. The other three describe feelings or outcomes without fixing any control.
Not quite. Only the sentence that names a control and a screen fixes the interface. The others describe outcomes or feelings, which stay conceptual.
