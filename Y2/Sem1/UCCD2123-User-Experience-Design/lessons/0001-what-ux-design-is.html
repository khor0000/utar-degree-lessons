What UX design is

User experience design (UXD) is designing a person's whole journey with a product or service, often across several products, so that it is usable and enjoyable, not only how one screen looks. The word that matters is journey. A journey is the sequence of steps someone takes towards a goal; the experience is what they live through along it, feelings included. The thing designed does not have to be physical, because an app or a service counts. A walk in the park has nothing designed in it, so it is not UX design's business. A journey is the steps, not the feeling: the feeling is measured along the steps, as the dots in the next diagram show.

You already know a close neighbour: when you polish a page in your own projects, you work on its screens. Keep that in mind, because the box below separates it from UX.

One journey, step by step

A student pays at a Westlake food stall with a QR e-wallet. Step through it and watch the feeling dots. Each place where a person meets a service is a touchpoint (Benyon ch4). Ordering GrabFood involves four: the app, a push notification, the rider at the gate and a bank payment.

The experience starts before any app opens: the student is in a queue, hoping the stall takes QR.

They open the wallet app. Quick, so the feeling holds.

They scan the stall's code. Going well.

The signal is weak and a spinner sits there, with people waiting behind. The feeling drops. The screen may look perfect and this still feels bad.

Payment succeeds. Relief.

They show the stall owner the success screen. The journey ends with another person, not on a screen.

The whole row is the user experience. Polishing only the screens would miss the queue, the signal and the stall owner.

Looks like UI design, but wider. Both care about screens. UI design shapes the interface. UX covers everything around it too, because the feeling comes from the whole journey. Benyon says it directly: "UX is not just a question of designing interfaces, however." The UI is one touchpoint, and UX covers the whole before, during and after. Norman and Nielsen make the same split: the total user experience differs from the user interface.

UI design needs an interface to work on. UX also covers a product with no screen of its own, if it is an interactive system. Benyon's parcel-tracking system (a web service, an app, vans, codes and readers) counts, while a chair does not, because it processes no content (Benyon ch1, 1.2).

The formal words

The slide (Lecture 1, p3) splits the term in two. User experience is the holistic journey users go through with a product while their understanding of it changes. Design is the discipline exploring the dialogue between products, users and contexts.

What UX designers work on is an interactive system: technology that responds to people's actions and handles content they can perceive. A phone app counts; a chair does not (Benyon, PDF p39).

Why Norman coined the term

The slide's answer: Don Norman coined "user experience" in 1988 to label the holistic journey users go through with a product while their perceptions change. It signifies that what counts is the whole journey, not one moment of use.

The textbook tells it differently: Norman adopted the term in 1993, on joining Apple (Benyon, Box 1.2, PDF p42). Write 1988 here, because T1 Q2 itself says 1988. It is on your ask-the-tutor list in PARKED.md.
