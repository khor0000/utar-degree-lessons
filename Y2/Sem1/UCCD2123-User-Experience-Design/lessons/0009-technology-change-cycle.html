Lesson 9 · Tutorial 1 Q8

Change the technology, and the rest changes

When technology changes, the activity and its context change too, which creates new requirements for technology, and the cycle repeats.

In Lesson 8 you changed the Contexts box. This lesson changes the Technologies box, and the effect keeps going around.

Two words first. Requirements are what a system must do and the qualities it should have. They are not a finished technology: they are what the activity asks of one. Opportunities are new possibilities a technology offers.

Watch one full turn

Start with an activity in its context: paying cash at a stall.

The activity sets requirements: payment must be fast, with no need for exact change.

A technology answers them: the QR e-wallet.

The technology offers opportunities: pay to the exact sen, with no wallet in your pocket.

So the activity itself changes. Students stop carrying cash, and phone battery now matters at the stall.

The changed activity sets new requirements: the owner must see proof that the money arrived. The cycle continues.

The textbook's example is bus travel: tracker displays and route apps changed what travelling by bus is (Benyon, Figure 2.1, PDF p55). "Technology" here includes communications and content, as well as hardware and software.

A common shorthand is P + A + C = T: technology should fit people, activities and contexts. It is a useful intuition, not an equation, because the cycle loops: technology offers opportunities that change activities, and with them contexts and people. The book's version is that activities and contexts "establish requirements for technologies that in turn offer opportunities that change the nature of activities" (Benyon ch2). A better shorthand is: P, A, C, then requirements, then T, then opportunities, then new A.

Designing technology to meet changed requirements is only half the cycle. New technology can also come first and create opportunities nobody asked for, as QR payment did for paying at a stall.

In the cycle, what do technologies give back to activities?

Right. Activities set requirements; technologies offer opportunities.
Requirements flow from activities to technologies. What comes back is opportunities.
Payment must work with no exact change.
Students can leave their wallets at home.
The owner must see proof the money arrived.
Prices can be paid to the exact sen.

Cycle (sample). Requirement: take attendance quickly in a big hall. Technology: projected QR code. Opportunity: students mark attendance by phone within seconds. Changed activity: no sheet passes around, and every student needs a working phone. New requirement: stop students scanning for absent friends.

Q8. A change in technology alters the nature of activities and contexts, and creates new requirements and opportunities. Activities establish requirements for technologies, and technologies offer opportunities that change those activities. The changed activities create new requirements, so the cycle continues. For example, QR payment turned paying at a stall into a phone-based activity.
