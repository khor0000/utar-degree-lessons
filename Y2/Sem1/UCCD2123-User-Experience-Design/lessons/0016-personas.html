Personas: one type of person, built from research

A persona is a concrete profile of one type of person the system is designed for, built from research data and PACT analysis. It helps the team design for that person's goals, instead of for themselves. It is not one real user, and it is not an average user.

Lesson 8a introduced the People list of a PACT analysis, and said a persona is built from that list. This lesson shows what the persona is for. The book gives the working definition: personas are "concrete representations of the different types of people that the system or service is being designed for" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84).

Why this matters. Every design decision needs an answer to the question "for whom?". A persona gives the team one clear answer to keep in mind while they design.

Focus on. What a persona is, where it comes from, and the two ways it helps the design process.

A persona is a built profile, and no real person stands behind it. The team uses it to think about a kind of user, and it has to be checked against real research.

Where a persona comes from

A persona starts from research, not from imagination. The lecture says that "personas are born from user research conducted in the Understanding phase" (Lecture 2, PDF p14). The chosen people are then described with the PACT elements: the people, their activities, their contexts and the technologies they use. The book says personas and scenarios are developed "using any of a wide range of methods of understanding and ideation, and through undertaking a PACT analysis" (Benyon ch3, chapter introduction, PDF p77).

The diagram follows the Sandy and Mari personas from the Health and Fitness Companion example, in the order a team would build them. Step through it.

Stage 1. The team gathers what real people do, want and struggle with. Personas are not invented from the team's own habits.

Stage 2. The data is sorted with PACT. Lesson 8a gave the same four elements. The People list becomes the source of the persona.

Stage 3. Sandy is one persona. Sandy "needs encouragement and motivation to exercise" (Lecture 2, PDF p16). Mari, the second persona, already has a fitness routine.

Stage 4. The persona is used to check a design. Because Sandy needs motivation, the team considers a reward system for completing exercise (Lecture 2, PDF p16). Mari, who enjoys exercise, would instead get personalized exercise plans (Lecture 2, PDF p19).

Two ways a persona helps

The first way is that a persona makes the team stop designing for itself. The book says: "Designers create personas so that they can envisage the people they are designing for; they create personas so that they can put themselves in other people's shoes" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84). The second way is that a persona makes different needs visible at once. Sandy and Mari differ, and the book says the Sandy persona "would need more encouragement and persuasion to exercise than the Mari persona" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p85).

Because one persona is one type of person, the team builds several. Each one has different goals, and the design must suit all of them, not one average person. The book warns against designers who "create personas that are just like themselves, or their ideal partner" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p85). Personas are also meant to change: the book says persona development "should be agile and subject to change" (PDF p85).

Two quick checks

Which statement about a persona is correct?

Right. A persona represents one type of person, and it comes from research data, not from one real user or from an average.
Not quite. A persona is not a real user, not an average, and not the designer. It is a profile of one type of person, built from research data.

Why does a team build several personas for one system?

Right. Several personas show several sets of goals, and the design has to suit all of them.
Not quite. Personas represent different types of people with different goals. One persona cannot stand in for all of them.
Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q7 on paper: What are personas, and how do they aid in the UX design process?

reveal model answer

Personas are concrete representations of the different types of people a system is designed for, built from research data and PACT analysis (people, activities, contexts and technologies). They capture the goals and aspirations of users as well as their functional needs, so the team can see the design from another person's point of view. They aid the design process by keeping the team designing for real user needs rather than its own assumptions, and by showing where different people need different features. Several personas are needed because different people have different goals, and a persona is neither a real user nor an average.

Primary source: Benyon, chapter 3, chapter introduction (PDF p77) and section 3.3 "Developing personas" (PDF p84 to p85). Lecture 2 PDF p14 to p16 for the research basis and the Sandy and Mari examples, and PDF p19 for the Mari suggestion.
