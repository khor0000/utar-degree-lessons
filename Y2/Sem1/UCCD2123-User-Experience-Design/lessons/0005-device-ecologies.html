From one device to a device ecology

A device ecology is a set of devices working together, so the experience happens across them rather than on one.

The word comes from nature: in an ecology, organisms work together to create an environment. Here the devices play that part (Benyon, PDF p36). A device ecology is a number of devices that "work together to create an environment" (Benyon ch1, 1.1). The devices can be the same type, such as two phones, or different types, such as a laptop and a projector. What matters is that they communicate and share content.

Watch one form

A single device: one person using one phone. UX was once studied like this.

Two phones in a café. Just having two devices is not yet an ecology.

Add a wi-fi connection, so they can communicate.

One person sends a photo to the other with AirDrop. The experience now spans all three: a miniature device ecology, which is the textbook's own example.

Looks like owning many devices, but different. A drawer of unconnected gadgets is a collection. It becomes an ecology only when the devices work together.

Tech adverts often say "ecosystem" for a similar idea inside one brand. An ecology does not need one brand, but it does need its devices to connect.

Why ecologies are hard

When an ecology does not connect smoothly, the result is "frustration and anger" and a poor UX (Benyon ch1, 1.1). That is why connection is a design problem, not only a technical one.

Tap a challenge.

The slide names four challenges (Lecture 1, p6). The explanations here are ours.

Interoperability: devices must connect and share content. An Android phone and an Apple Watch may not.

Consistency: the same task should feel alike on each device, such as your phone and laptop.

Privacy and security: data moving between devices can reach the wrong person.

Complexity: more devices means more setup and more places where things can go wrong.

Q4. UX is often experienced through an ecology of devices rather than a single device. A device ecology is a number of devices working together to create an environment, such as two iPhones and a wi-fi connection sharing a photo. Designers must therefore consider the experience across all the devices. This raises challenges of interoperability, UX consistency, data privacy and security, and complexity of use.
