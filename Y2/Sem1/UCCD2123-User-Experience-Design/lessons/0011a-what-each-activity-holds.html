What each activity holds

Understanding produces requirements, design produces a conceptual and a physical design, envisionment makes those designs visible, and evaluation checks them. The boxes overlap, so the same tool can appear in more than one.

Lesson 11 gave four names. This lesson says what goes inside each one.

Why this matters. Every later lesson sits inside one of these four boxes. Knowing what a box holds tells you which lesson you are in.

Focus on. What each activity holds, and the difference between a need and a requirement.

How you'll be tested. Tutorial 2 Q1, in writing, closed book.

Looks like Lesson 9's "requirements", but it differs in where the word comes from. Lesson 9 used it for what an activity asks of a technology. Here it is what understanding produces, from research with people.

Understanding. A PACT analysis, the needs people report, and the requirements drawn from them.

Design. Ideas first, then conceptual design (what the system is for) and physical design (how it works and looks).

Envisionment. The sketches, storyboards and prototypes that make the design visible to the designer and to people.

Evaluation. Checks each of them, the requirements list included. Its results feed the next round.

Understanding holds PACT and requirements

Understanding is "concerned with what the system or service has to do, what it has to be like and how it has to fit in with other things" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p78). It starts with PACT: "before creative design can start it is essential that the designer develops a clear and thorough understanding of" the PACT elements (Benyon ch7, Aims, PDF p175). So a PACT analysis belongs to understanding. Lesson 8a shows how to do one.

PACT is not only a snapshot of today. A PACT analysis is "useful for both analysis and design activities: understanding the current situation, seeing where possible improvements can be made or envisioning future situations" (Benyon ch2, 2.6, PDF p72).

Needs become requirements

Need: what people want, or what they lack, in what they do now or would like to do. The book does not define it formally; it writes of "needs and desires" (Benyon ch3, 3.1, PDF p77). Benyon ch3, 3.1; ch7, PDF p176

A requirement is the next step. Designers "will express these wants and desires as requirements", and a requirement is "something the product must do or a quality that the product must have" (Benyon ch7, PDF p176).

The need comes first. Lesson 9 has the same direction, where a changed activity created new requirements. The difference is the cause: there the world changed, and here the designer finds the needs. The book says "requirements are essentially about understanding" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79).

Understanding is many needs, and it keeps going

One session does not find one need. The book names several sources in one passage: stakeholders, "observations of existing systems", and "what people do now and what they would like to do" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79). Which methods to use is Lesson 13's job. Understanding also continues after design starts, because it is "rarely possible to acquire a thorough understanding of requirements until some design work has been completed and evaluated" (Benyon ch7, Aims, PDF p175).

Design decides; envisionment shows

Design holds the creative step, which the book calls "idea generation" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79). It then decides twice. Conceptual design is "about designing a system in the abstract", so it decides what the system is for. Physical design "is concerned with making things concrete", so it decides how things work and look (PDF p79 and p81). Lesson 14 covers the difference.

You might reasonably wonder why envisionment is needed once the look is decided. A decision on paper is hard for other people to react to. The book says designs "need to be visualized both to help designers clarify their ideas and to enable people to evaluate them" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p82). Envisionment makes the decision visible, as a sketch, a storyboard or a prototype. Conceptual design needs it too.

Evaluation checks all of them

"Any of the other design activities will be followed by an evaluation" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p83). That check can be "simply the designer checking through", or a check of "a list of requirements" sent to a client. It is not only a formal test with users.

Which statement about what Understanding holds is correct?

Right. Understanding includes PACT, the needs and the requirements. PACT is also used later, to envision the future.
Not quite. Understanding holds PACT analysis, needs and requirements. Conceptual design belongs to design, and PACT is not only a snapshot of now.

Right. A decision is one thing; making it visible so people can evaluate it is envisionment.
Not quite. Physical design is decided, not invisible. Envisionment renders the decision so that people can react to it.
Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson. Take the campus food-ordering app from Lesson 11. In one line each, write what Understanding, Design, Envisionment and Evaluation hold for it. Then answer T2 Q1 in two sentences: what are the four main activities?

reveal model answer

Understanding: a PACT analysis of students, ordering, the canteen and phones; the needs (students queue, and some have no cash); the requirement (order and pay from a phone in under a minute). Design: ideas, then a conceptual design (menu, order, pay, pick-up time) and a physical design (a large Pay button at the bottom). Envisionment: a sketch or clickable prototype of the order screens. Evaluation: the checks on each, such as three students trying the prototype, or the team checking the requirements list.

T2 Q1. The four activities are understanding (what the system must do, and how it fits with other things), design (conceptual and physical), envisionment (making design visible) and evaluation (checking them with people, at every step).
