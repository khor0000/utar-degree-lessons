The P and the persona: how many, and why not just the research

In PACT there is one P, the real people you studied. A persona is the design tool you build from that research. A 30-row People list gives a few personas, grouped by goals, not 30 personas. A persona does not replace the research.

Lesson 16a showed where personas are used. This lesson answers the two questions that follow: how many personas to make, and why a team needs a persona at all if it already studies real people.

Looks like the People list from Lesson 8a, but it differs. The list is the variety of people, many rows. A persona is one profile that summarises some of those rows, and it is a tool for designing and discussing.

The P and the persona

The P (real people)A persona
What it isReal people, with real activities, contexts and technologiesA fictional profile of one type of person
Where it comes fromInterviews, observation and other researchSummarised from the P, using PACT
What it is forFinding out what people do and struggle withLetting the team design and discuss for one specific person

The lecture calls personas fictional, archetypal characters built on real user research and data (Lecture 2, PDF p14). So the P is the evidence, and the persona is the tool built from it.

How many personas to make

A People list of 30 rows does not mean 30 personas. The book gives no fixed number. It says that a new system is likely to be used by different types of people, so it is important to develop several different personas (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84).

Working rule (not in the book): group the People rows by their goals, not by each row. Rows with the same goals can share one persona, and rows with different goals need separate personas.

Why a persona when PACT already studies real people

A 30-row list shows the variety, but a team cannot hold thirty variants in mind while it makes one decision. A persona packages the findings into one person with goals, so the team can ask what this person would do. You might reasonably wonder whether the persona replaces the research. It does not. The book says persona development should be agile and subject to change (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p85), so the research keeps checking the persona.

Produce it • closed book • Tutorial 2 Q7

Close lessons 16 to 16b. Answer Q7 on paper: What are personas, and how do they aid UX design? Name one activity that uses personas, say how many personas a 30-row People list should give, and explain why personas do not replace the research.

reveal model answer

Personas are concrete representations of the different types of people a system is designed for, built from research data and PACT analysis (people, activities, contexts and technologies). They capture the goals and aspirations of users as well as their functional needs, so the team can see the design from another person's point of view. They aid the design process by keeping the team designing for real user needs rather than its own assumptions, and by showing where different people need different features. Personas are used in understanding, envisionment, design and evaluation; for example, an expert walks through a task as one persona. A 30-row People list does not give 30 personas: rows that share goals are grouped, so the team gets a few personas, one for each type of person with different goals. A persona does not replace the research. It packages the research so the team can discuss one specific person, and the research keeps checking it.

Primary source: Benyon, chapter 3, section 3.3 (PDF p84 to p85). Lecture 2, PDF p14 to p16.
