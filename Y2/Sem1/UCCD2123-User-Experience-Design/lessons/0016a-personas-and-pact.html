PACT and personas: one P, summarised into a persona

PACT feeds personas. The research on real people, the P in PACT, is summarised into a few fictional personas, and the team uses those personas in several UX activities. A persona is not a second kind of People data, and it is not a participant.

Lesson 16 said a persona is built from research and PACT. This lesson covers where personas are used, how many to make, and why a team needs them if it already studies real people.

Where personas are used

Personas are used in more than one UX activity. The book says scenarios "and their associated personas" are a core technique, useful for "understanding, envisioning, evaluation, and both conceptual and physical design" (Benyon ch3, 3.4, PDF p89). The diagram follows one persona through the process.

Stage 1, understanding. Real people are studied, and PACT lists the variety of people, activities, contexts and technologies. This is where the data about people comes from.

Stage 2, the persona. The team summarises that research into a few personas. Each persona is a fictional profile of one type of person, grounded in the data.

Stage 3, design. Scenarios use the persona as the character doing the activity. The team asks whether each design idea serves that persona's goals.

Stage 4, evaluation. In expert-based evaluation the expert "may adopt one of the personas" (Benyon ch10, 10.3, PDF p275). The expert is not a real user.

The lecture gives the same split. Personas are "born from user research conducted in the Understanding phase", and they then "directly inform and validate design decisions" in the design and envisionment phase (Lecture 2, PDF p14).

How PACT feeds a persona

PACT is not only a snapshot of today. The book says a PACT analysis is "useful for both analysis and design activities: understanding the current situation, seeing where possible improvements can be made or envisioning future situations". It is also "useful for developing personas and scenarios" (Benyon ch2, 2.6, PDF p72).

There is one P, not two. The P is the real people you studied. A persona is the design tool you build from that P. The lecture calls personas "fictional, archetypal characters" based on "real user research and data" (Lecture 2, PDF p14). The table keeps the two apart.

What it isReal people, with real activities, contexts and technologiesA fictional profile of one type of person

How many personas?

A People list of 30 rows does not mean 30 personas. The book gives no fixed number. It says that because a new system is likely to be used by different types of people, "it is important to develop several different personas" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84). Its website example has four: a school teacher in Germany, a university lecturer from the UK, a child in Africa and a Stevenson enthusiast from the USA (same page).

Working rule (not stated in the book): group the People rows by their goals, not by each row. Two rows with the same goals and different ages can share one persona. Two rows with different goals need two. The book's four personas differ in "very different goals and aspirations" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84), and Cooper's goal types (experience, end and life goals) are the kinds the book says to represent (same page).

Why personas, if PACT already studies real people?

The People list shows variety, and a team cannot hold thirty variants in mind while it makes one decision. A persona packages the findings into one person with goals, so the team can ask "what would this person do?" That is the book's reason: "Designers create personas so that they can envisage the people they are designing for; they create personas so that they can put themselves in other people's shoes" (Benyon ch3, 3.3, PDF p84).

You might reasonably wonder whether the persona replaces the research. It does not: persona development "should be agile and subject to change" (PDF p85). Once a team has characters, its members start to talk about them: "what will happen when the grandmother tries to use it?" (Benyon ch9, 9.4, PDF p249).

Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q7 on paper: what are personas, and how do they aid UX design? In your answer, name one activity that uses personas, say how many personas a 30-row People list should give, and explain why personas do not replace the research.

reveal model answer

Personas are concrete representations of the different types of people a system is designed for. They are fictional profiles built from research data, and they are developed through a PACT analysis. They aid design by letting the team envisage and design for people other than themselves, and they are used in understanding, envisionment, design and evaluation. For example, an expert walks through a task as one persona. A 30-row People list does not give 30 personas: rows that share goals are grouped, so the team gets a few personas, one for each type of person with different goals. A persona does not replace the research. It packages the research so the team can discuss one specific person, and the research keeps checking it.
