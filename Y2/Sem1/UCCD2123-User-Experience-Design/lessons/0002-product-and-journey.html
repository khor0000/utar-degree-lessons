Product and journey, pinned down

The product is the thing you are responsible for designing. A journey is what one person goes through to reach one goal. A journey is a sequence of interactions over time, taken to get something done. Benyon uses the same idea for a service: "a sequence of interactions that constitutes a whole, more abstract achievement" (ch4).

The slides use both words without a tight definition, so these are working meanings to help you think. Do not quote them as definitions in an exam.

Product and journey are not the same size

The product is what you own, so its boundary is set by your job. The journey is what the person goes through, and it can cross several products that you do not own. In the three jobs above, the student's journey stays the same while the product you design changes.

A product holds one or more touchpoints: the places where a person meets it. So designing a product means designing its share of the journey well. Designing a journey means making all the touchpoints, across products, fit together into one coherent experience. The book describes this bigger picture as the moments linking into strings of interactions, which it calls the whole user journey (ch4, 4.2). Lesson 6b covers touchpoints in detail.

Only interactive things count as products

A product does not have to be physical: apps, websites and services count. A painting on a wall does not respond to people, so it is out of scope. A gallery's audio-guide app or ticket kiosk is in scope, because the book's list of interactive systems includes "a tourist attraction" that guides visitors (Benyon ch1, 1.2).
