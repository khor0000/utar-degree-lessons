Envisionment: making ideas visible

Envisionment is the activity that turns a design idea into something people can see, such as a sketch or a prototype, so that the team can think about it, explore it and evaluate it. It is not the final, polished mock-up.

Lesson 11 named four activities, and envisionment was one of them. This lesson explains its job. The book defines it as "concerned with making ideas visible; with externalizing thoughts" (Benyon ch8, Aims, PDF p211). Externalizing means getting a thought out of your head and onto something that can be looked at, changed and shared.

Why this matters. Each round of the loop in Lesson 11 needs something to react to. Envisionment is the activity that produces it.

Nothing you have met works quite like it yet: a finished-looking screen tends to read as a decision already made. Envisionment output is meant to be doubted, changed and sometimes thrown away.

What a rough form does

An envisioned form does three jobs, and the lecture lists the same three (Lecture 7, PDF p4 and p5). It brings abstract ideas to life. It exposes flaws and difficulties. It generates new ideas. The book states the first job like this: "It is easy to have great ideas in your head, but it is only by envisioning them that the flaws and difficulties will be exposed" (Benyon ch8, 8.2, PDF p213).

The diagram follows one idea from the campus food-ordering app in Lesson 11: "order and pay in under a minute". Step through it. Each stage adds one thing, and the caption says what it did.

Stage 1. The idea exists only in one head. Nobody else can test it, so its flaw stays hidden.

Stage 2. A sketch takes minutes to draw and costs nothing to throw away. Drawing it already raises a layout question: where does Pay go?

Stage 3. Three students look at the paper version. Two cannot find the button that pays. This is an illustrative example, not a result from the sources.

Stage 4. The team moves Pay to the bottom and draws version 2 beside version 1. A paper change costs minutes. The lecture notes that a detailed prototype is time-consuming to develop (Lecture 7, PDF p26), so the cheap version is the one to change.

Why the rough form comes first

Envisionment is not the same as making the final polished mock-up. The book's test is whether a representation suits its purpose: "A sketch 'on the back of an envelope' might be useful for generating an idea and expressing it to a colleague – but it is not so good for giving to a client" (Benyon ch8, 8.1, PDF p212). Sketches are "quick, timely, inexpensive, disposable and plentiful" (ch8, 8.2, PDF p213), and a lo-fi prototype "should be produced quickly and discarded easily" (Lecture 7, PDF p27). A polished version has its place, but usually later and for an outside audience.

The book's own summary lists the same basic techniques: "storyboards, different forms of sketch, mood boards, maps, wireframes, lo-fi and hi-fi prototypes" (Benyon ch8, Summary, PDF p235). It also warns that no single technique "will lead to the perfect design" (ch8, 8.1, PDF p212).

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q5 on paper: What is the role of envisionment in UX design, and what techniques are commonly used?

reveal model answer

Envisionment is the activity of making design ideas visible, such as sketches, storyboards, mood boards, wireframes and lo-fi or hi-fi prototypes, so that the designer and the people who will use the system can think about the ideas, explore alternatives and evaluate them. It brings abstract ideas to life, exposes flaws and generates new ideas, and it is how the team communicates with colleagues and clients. Commonly used techniques are sketching, storyboarding, mood boards, maps, wireframes and prototypes, including Wizard of Oz prototypes in which a human plays the missing feature. The representation should suit its purpose, so a polished version is not always needed.
