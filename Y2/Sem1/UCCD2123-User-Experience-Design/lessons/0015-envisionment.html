Envisionment: making ideas visible

Envisionment turns a design idea into something people can see, such as a sketch or a prototype, so that the team can think about it, explore it and evaluate it. It is not the final, polished mock-up.

Lesson 11 named envisionment as one of the four activities, and Lesson 11b separated it from physical design. This lesson explains what envisionment is for. The book defines it as making ideas visible, which means externalising thoughts (Benyon ch8, Aims, PDF p211). Externalising means getting a thought out of your head and onto something that can be looked at, changed and shared.

Looks like the envisionment activity in Lesson 11, but it differs in what it is for. Lesson 11 named the activity. This lesson gives its reason: a finished-looking screen tends to read as a decision already made, while envisioned output is meant to be doubted and changed.

What a rough form does

An envisioned form does three jobs. It brings abstract ideas to life, it exposes flaws and difficulties, and it generates new ideas (Lecture 7, PDF p4). The book makes the same point: an idea in your head hides its flaws until you envision it (Benyon ch8, 8.2, PDF p213).

The diagram follows one idea from the campus food-ordering app: order and pay in under a minute. Step through it.

Stage 1. The idea exists only in one head. Nobody else can test it, so its flaw stays hidden.

Stage 2. A sketch takes minutes to draw and costs nothing to throw away. Drawing it already raises a layout question: where does Pay go?

Stage 3. Three students look at the paper version. Two cannot find the button that pays. This is an illustrative example, not a result from the sources.

Stage 4. The team moves Pay to the bottom and draws version 2 beside version 1. A paper change costs minutes, so the cheap version is the one to change.
