Usability: why all three fields care

Usability is the ease with which users can effectively and efficiently interact with a system to achieve their goals (Lecture 4, p11).

The book's wording is that usability "refers to the quality of the interaction", judged by the time taken to perform tasks, the number of errors made and the time to become a competent user (Benyon ch5).

See the definition

Start with a person who wants something: say, to pay for lunch.

They use a system: the e-wallet.

The system exists to get them to a goal: the stall is paid.

Effective: the system provides the functions needed, so the goal can be reached.

Efficient: the goal is reached with minimal effort or time.

Effective looks like efficient, but they differ. Paying after nine screens is effective but not efficient. A one-tap button that fails is neither.

The payment works after twelve taps and three retries.
Scan once and the payment goes through.
The app opens instantly but has no way to pay stalls.
A search bar finds the right product in seconds.

The rest of the list

The slides say a highly usable system has five characteristics: efficient, effective, safe to use, easy to learn, and high utility (Lecture 4, p11). Utility means it does something useful.

Why all three share it

Every field works through interactions, so every field cares, in its own way. HCI measures usability, ID designs for it, and UX depends on it. The book puts it plainly: "Usability underlies good UX" (Benyon ch5).

Every field needs people to reach their goals, so if a system fails here, each field's work fails too.

UX: a person who cannot finish a task will not have a positive experience.

HCI: it studies interaction, judged by efficiency and effectiveness, the same words as usability.

ID: a design only succeeds if people can actually use its interactions.

Take a QR payment at a kopitiam. HCI counts the taps and errors it takes. ID cuts the taps and decides what the screen shows next. UX depends on the whole payment feeling quick and safe.
