Evaluation at the centre

Evaluation means checking a design idea, software, product or service against some criteria, and it happens at every stage of the UX design process, not only at the end.

Picture evaluation as one test near the end of a project, and the picture is wrong. The book says "everything gets evaluated at every step of the process" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p78). The lecture says the same in its own words: evaluation "can occur at any stage of development, from early concepts to fully implemented systems" (Lecture 9, PDF p3). The book's rule is that "any of the other design activities will be followed by an evaluation" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p83).

Why this matters. Every design you draft in the later lessons gets checked by one of these methods. Knowing which method fits which stage tells you what to do with a draft.

Focus on. The four stages where evaluation happens, and the three families of method and how they differ.

Looks like the testing step at the end of a software project, but it differs. A final test checks finished code. UX evaluation starts with a paper sketch, long before anything is built, and it repeats at every stage.

Where evaluation happens

Step through the campus food-ordering app from Lesson 11, one stage at a time. The lecture names four stages, from initial concepts to finished product (Lecture 9, PDF p4). Each stage uses its own kind of evaluation, and the bottom band shows that evaluation runs through all of them.

Initial concepts. A paper prototype of the order screen is compared with a competitor app (Lecture 9, PDF p4).

Design options. Two menu layouts are compared in a quick experiment. Once the app is live, A/B testing does this job: real users are randomly split between two versions (Lecture 9, PDF p4; Benyon ch10, PDF p275).

Usability testing. Three students try the prototype. This is formative evaluation, so the results shape the next version (Lecture 9, PDF p4; Benyon ch10, 10.1, PDF p271).

Finished product. The released app is checked against a standard such as ISO 9241. That is summative evaluation (Lecture 9, PDF p4). Data analytics then tracks real use.

Two stage words need a definition. Formative evaluation is done while the design is still changing, so its results help to form it. Summative evaluation is done on a finished product, to judge it against performance criteria or a usability standard (Lecture 9, PDF p4).

Three families of method, compared

The book sorts evaluation methods into three types: expert-based, participant-based and data analytics (Benyon ch10, 10.1, PDF p268). The lecture uses the same three (Lecture 9, PDF p4). They differ in who does the checking and when.

FamilyWho does the checkingUsers' roleTypical methods
Expert-basedA usability expert or UX designer, reviewing an envisioned designNot neededHeuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough
Participant-basedPeople recruited to use an envisioned designReal people use itUser testing, cooperative evaluation, controlled experiments
Data analyticsData gathered once the system is deployedLive use dataA/B testing, page views, heat maps

The trade-off is speed against realism. Expert-based methods "will often pick up significant usability or UX issues quickly, but experts will sometimes miss detailed issues that real users find difficult" (Benyon ch10, 10.1, PDF p268). That is why participant methods "must be used at some point in the development process" (same page). Heuristic evaluation is formative, not summative (Benyon ch10, 10.3, PDF p277).

Two quick checks

Which statement about when evaluation happens is correct?

Right. Evaluation runs at every stage, from the first sketch to the finished product.
Not quite. Evaluation is not a single end test. It runs at every stage, so an early sketch is checked too.

A team wants to know which of two versions of a live checkout page gets more clicks. Which method fits?

Right. A/B testing compares two live versions using real use data.
Not quite. The team needs real use data from a live page, and that is data analytics. The other three use an envisioned design, not live traffic.
Produce it • closed book

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q2 and T2 Q6 on paper: Why is evaluation central to the UX design process, and at what stages does it occur? What are some methods used in evaluating UX designs, and how do they differ?

reveal model answer

Evaluation is central because everything gets evaluated at every step of the process, so a design is checked against its criteria from the first idea to the finished product. It occurs at initial concepts (paper prototypes compared with competitor products), at design options (quick experiments comparing alternatives), at usability testing (formative, with users trying a prototype) and at the finished product (summative, against a standard such as ISO 9241, plus data analytics after deployment). The main methods are expert-based (heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough), which are quick but can miss problems real users have; participant-based (user testing), which gives a realistic picture from real people; and data analytics (such as A/B testing), which uses data from live use. They differ in who does the checking and when.
