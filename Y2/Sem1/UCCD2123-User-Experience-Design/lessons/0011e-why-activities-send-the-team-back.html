Why any activity can send the team back

The process is iterative because evaluation finds problems, and each problem points to the activity that caused it. The team goes back to that activity, repairs the design, and evaluates again. Each round refines the design rather than restarting it.

Lesson 11d said the process has no fixed order. This lesson shows the return path, and why the return is a round rather than a restart.

Looks like a parcel sent back to the shop that packed it, not to the start of the whole order. The return goes to the activity that caused the problem, and the rest of the design stays.

One problem, one return

Take a pick-up screen. Round 1 is a design, and the test shows that students cannot find their tracking number. That problem points to design, so the team returns there. Round 2 changes the screen, and the next test shows the fix works. Step through it.

Round 1, design. The first pick-up screen asks for the tracking number before anything else.

Round 1, evaluation. Three of four students cannot find their tracking number. That is a problem with the design.

Back to design. The problem sends the team back to the activity that caused it.

Round 2, design. The screen asks for the phone number first, then lists the parcels.

Round 2, evaluation. All four students find their parcel in under 20 seconds. One asks whether a text can say when it is ready.

Back to understanding. That new need starts the next round.

Why the return is repeated

The lecture gives the same reason in other words: UX design is a cyclic method that keeps learning about users and refining the design (Lecture 2, PDF p3). The book says both the understanding of needs and the design solutions evolve during UX design (Benyon ch3, 3.1, PDF p77). So the team returns to any activity, and each return refines the design.

Starting in the middle

The process can also begin partway through. The lecture gives three starting points, each with a first move (Lecture 2, PDF p13).

StartExampleFirst move
DesignA finished visual design is handed overEvaluate it with usability testing, to find its flaws
EvaluationThe live app has poor user reviewsEvaluate the live app, before designing a fix
EnvisionmentA rough prototype shows a feature is possibleTest the prototype with users, then make the official design

Produce it • closed book • Tutorial 2 Q1

Close these lessons (11 to 11e). Answer Q1 on paper: What are the four main activities in the UX design process, and why is the process considered iterative? Use one example that starts from evaluation.

reveal model answer

The four main activities are understanding (finding out what the system must do and how it fits with other things), design (conceptual and physical design), envisionment (making ideas visible, such as prototypes) and evaluation, which is central and happens after every activity. The process is iterative, not linear: it can start at any activity, the activities can happen in any order, and any problem found sends the team back to the activity that caused it. For example, a team starts from an app with poor reviews (evaluation), finds the menu layout is the problem, returns to design, builds a prototype (envisionment) and evaluates again.

Primary source: Benyon, chapter 3, sections 3.1 and 3.2 (PDF p77 to p78). Lecture 2, PDF p3 and p13. Lecture 6, PDF p3 for the iterative wording on requirements.
