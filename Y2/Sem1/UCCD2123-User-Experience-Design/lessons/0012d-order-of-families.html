Which families to use, and in what order

The book does not say every project must use all three families. For a simple project it combines expert-based and participant methods, running the expert review first. Data analytics comes last, because it needs a running system.

Lessons 12b and 12c described the three families. This lesson shows how a team puts them in order.

Looks like tasting a dish: the chef checks the recipe on paper, then a few guests taste it, then the restaurant tracks what customers order. Each check needs the thing it tests to exist yet.

What the book recommends

The book says participant methods must be used at some point (Benyon ch10, 10.1, PDF p268). For a simple project, it recommends combining expert-based and participant methods, and running the expert review first, so that its results help to focus the participant testing (Benyon ch10, 10.5, PDF p284).

Sketch or mock-up. An expert reviews the idea before users see it. Experts pick up significant problems quickly (Benyon ch10, PDF p268).

Working prototype. Real people try it, because experts sometimes miss detailed problems that real users find difficult (Benyon ch10, PDF p268).

Complete product. A summative check against criteria, or a statistical evaluation of the near-complete product in its intended setting (Benyon ch10, PDF p268).

Live system. Data analytics runs on real use. A/B testing randomly assigns users to two versions (Benyon ch10, PDF p275).

Working rule (not in the book). Use expert review on a sketch, participant tests on a prototype, and analytics once the system is live. Expert review is a first step, but the book says it will not find all problems (Benyon ch10, PDF p278), so it does not replace participant tests.

Produce it • closed book • Tutorial 2 Q2 and Q6

Close lessons 12 to 12d. Answer Q2 and Q6 on paper: Why is evaluation central to the UX design process, and at what stages does it occur? What are some methods used in evaluating UX designs, and how do they differ?

reveal model answer

Evaluation is central because everything gets evaluated at every step of the process, so a design is checked against its criteria from the first idea to the finished product. It occurs at initial concepts (paper prototypes compared with competitor products), at design options (quick experiments comparing alternatives), at usability testing (formative, with users trying a prototype) and at the finished product (summative, against a standard such as ISO 9241, plus data analytics after deployment). The main methods are expert-based (heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough), which are quick and cheap and need no users, but can miss problems real users have; participant-based (user testing), which gives a realistic picture from real people but is slower and costlier; and data analytics (such as A/B testing), which needs a live system and shows what people do, not why. They differ in who does the checking and when.

Primary source: Benyon, chapter 10, sections 10.1, 10.3 and 10.5 (PDF p268, p277, p284). Lecture 9, PDF p3, p4 and p13.
