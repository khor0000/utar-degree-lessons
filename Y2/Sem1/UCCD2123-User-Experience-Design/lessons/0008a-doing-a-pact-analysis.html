Doing a PACT analysis

The UX designer does a PACT analysis, at the start of a design problem, to list the variety of People, Activities, Contexts and Technologies.

In Lesson 8 you met the four boxes with one example each. A PACT analysis is the full version. The designer lists many items under each box, for one real situation, before deciding what to design.

Lesson 8's table gave one example per box. An analysis lists the whole range of each box, and it ends with trade-offs, which the table never does.

Who does it

The UX designer does it, not HCI researchers. PACT is a designer's framework in Benyon. HCI research supplies knowledge about people and technology, and the designer draws on that knowledge. Lesson 6 shows how HCI differs from design.

When to do it

At the start, to scope a design problem. To scope a problem means to mark its edges: which people, activities, contexts and technologies the design must cover. The book says a PACT analysis serves both analysis and design: understanding the current situation, finding possible improvements, or envisioning future situations (ch2, 2.6). You don't wait for someone to report a problem first.

What it produces

The designer simply lists the variety of each element that is possible, or likely, in a domain (ch2, 2.6). A domain is one area of activity the design serves. Working rule (not in the book): one analysis per domain, not per product. If a journey uses two apps, one analysis lists both under Technologies. A domain can hold several journeys, for different people or goals, and one analysis can cover them all.

The output is four headed lists, not a branching journey map. Here is one for paying at a kopitiam with Touch 'n Go:

ElementVariety for paying at a kopitiam with Touch 'n Go
PeopleStudents, aunties with poor eyesight, foreign students
ActivitiesPay a small amount quickly, often
ContextsNoisy, crowded, weak signal
TechnologiesPhone camera, QR sticker, mobile data

Then look for trade-offs: a gain on one side that costs something on another. Here speed and security pull against each other. A PIN check protects the money but slows a RM3 payment. The book tells the designer to look for trade-offs between combinations of PACT elements (ch2, 2.6).

Why it helps

The lists feed later design work. The book also uses the analysis to build personas and scenarios (ch2, 2.6). A persona is a profile of one type of user, built from research data and the People list. It is not one real person, and not an average user. A scenario is a short story of that persona doing an activity in a context. It is not the one-line user story that software teams write for developers.

Produce it • closed book

Fresh case: borrowing a book from the UTAR library. Write one four-heading PACT analysis, with at least two items under each heading. Then name one trade-off you noticed.

reveal model answer

People: first-year students new to the catalogue; final-year students who know it well; students with weak eyesight. Activities: search the catalogue; collect or return a book at the counter; renew a loan. Contexts: quiet zones; crowds before exams; the library's opening hours. Technologies: student ID card scan; self-service kiosk screen; library website on a phone. Trade-off: a self-service kiosk saves staff time, but first-year students need on-screen help to use it.
