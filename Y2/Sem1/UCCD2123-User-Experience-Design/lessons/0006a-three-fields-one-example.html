HCI, ID and UX design on one product

This lesson takes one product, a Touch 'n Go payment, and looks at it through three fields: human-computer interaction (HCI), interaction design (ID) and UX design. Interaction design and UX design overlap heavily, and they differ in focus, not size. HCI is a third field, and it overlaps both.

In Lesson 6 you met the line experience, study, design. Working rule (not in the book): that line is a memory hook. It is not a book definition.

Interaction design is not just a longer name for UX design. The book treats them as different fields with real overlap, and it refuses a tidy split between them.

Three fields, in the book's words

HCI arose in the early 1980s, and the book defines it as the design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use (Benyon ch1, evolving nature). Much of its work is studies that measure and compare.

ID was an established discipline in its own right by the mid-2000s (ch1). Benyon defines it as "concerned with the allocation of functions to human agency or to technology and with the structuring and sequencing of the interactions" (ch3, 3.2). Plainly: ID decides what the phone does, what the person does, and in what order.

UX design is the wider practice aimed at the whole experience. Don Norman adopted the term "user experience" in 1993 (the slide says 1988; see Lesson 1). The book describes itself as being about human-centred UX design, and also about HCI and interaction design. So the book sits across all three, and the overlap is real.

One kopitiam payment, three ways

Take Touch 'n Go eWallet paying at a kopitiam.

HCI: a researcher compares error rates for typing a merchant number and scanning a QR code, then publishes the findings.

ID: a designer decides the sequence (open app, scan, check amount, PIN or biometric) and what the phone does versus what the person does.

UX design: someone designs the whole journey: hearing about the wallet, topping up at 7-Eleven, paying, the receipt, refunds, customer support and feeling safe.

All three care about people, interactive technology and usability. The difference is focus: HCI studies how people use systems, ID designs the interactions, and UX design aims at the whole experience.

Watch the findings move

HCI is a field of studies. A researcher measures error rates for two ways of paying, then publishes the results.

ID designs interactions at both scales: a single microinteraction and, per Chapter 4 (4.2), the whole user journey. UX design's focus is the whole experience, which is why the two circles overlap heavily (see Lesson 6c).

The findings feed both. The interaction designer and the experience designer each use the same study, with their own focus.

A team checks that topping up, paying and refunds feel positive as one experience. Which field?

Right. Making the whole experience positive across the steps is UX design.
Not quite. Topping up, paying and refunds form one experience, which UX design aims at.

A lab measures error rates for two input methods and publishes the results. Which field?

Right. Comparing error rates in a study is HCI.
Not quite. Measuring and publishing error rates is a study, so it is HCI.

reveal model answer

HCI: a researcher tests whether passengers mis-tap the pickup pin on the map, then publishes the error rate.
