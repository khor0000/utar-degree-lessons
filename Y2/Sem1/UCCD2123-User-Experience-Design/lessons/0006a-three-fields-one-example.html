HCI, ID and UX design on one product

This lesson takes one product, a Touch 'n Go payment, and looks at it through three fields: human-computer interaction (HCI), interaction design (ID) and UX design. Interaction design and UX design overlap, but UX design is the wider circle. HCI is a third field, and it overlaps both.

In Lesson 6 you met the line goal, study, design. Working rule (not in the book): that line is a memory hook. It is not a book definition.

Interaction design is not just a longer name for UX design. The book treats them as different fields with real overlap, and it refuses a tidy split between them.

Three fields, in the book's words

HCI arose in the early 1980s. It is "concerned with the design, evaluation, and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use" (Benyon ch1, evolving nature). Much of its work is studies that measure and compare.

ID was "firmly established as a discipline in its own right" by the mid-2000s. Benyon defines it as "concerned with the allocation of functions to human agency or to technology and with the structuring and sequencing of the interactions" (ch3, 3.2). Plainly: ID decides what the phone does, what the person does, and in what order.

UX design is the activity of designing the whole experience. The term "user experience" was adopted by Don Norman in 1993. The book describes itself as "about human-centred UX design", and it also says it is "about human–computer interaction and interaction design (ID)". So the book sits across all three, and the overlap is real.

One kopitiam payment, three ways

Take Touch 'n Go eWallet paying at a kopitiam.

HCI: a researcher compares error rates for typing a merchant number and scanning a QR code, then publishes the findings.

ID: a designer decides the sequence (open app, scan, check amount, PIN or biometric) and what the phone does versus what the person does.

UX design: someone designs the whole journey: hearing about the wallet, topping up at 7-Eleven, paying, the receipt, refunds, customer support and feeling safe.

All three care about people, interactive technology and usability. The difference is scope: one study, one sequence of interactions, or the whole journey.

Watch the findings move

HCI is a field of studies. A researcher measures error rates for two ways of paying, then publishes the results.

ID works on one interaction inside the whole journey, which is the wider UX design circle.

The findings feed both. The ID designer and the journey planner each use the same study, at their own scale.

HCI: a researcher tests whether passengers mis-tap the pickup pin on the map, then publishes the error rate.
