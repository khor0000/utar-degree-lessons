Mood boards and design workbooks: two different records

A mood board collects images, colours and textures to set the look and feel of a design. A design workbook collects the design's own proposals and materials, kept through a project to investigate options. One records the feeling; the other records the design's history.

Lesson 15a listed mood boards among the techniques, and it did not cover design workbooks. This lesson separates the two, because they look alike: both are collections of visual material pinned up or kept in one place.

Looks like two boards in a campus club room. One is a pinboard of inspiration: photos and colours to set the mood. The other is a project folder: the club's own proposals, with the ones that failed kept beside the ones that worked.

Mood board: how it should feel

Designers gather visual stimuli that capture the feeling of the design: photographs, colours, textures, shapes, quotations and clips (Benyon ch8, 8.3, PDF p217). The book's rule is that anything goes. The board is not a formal representation of the design. It is inspiration, which can start a line of thought or suggest a colour scheme (Benyon ch8, PDF p218). The lecture lists five roles a mood board plays, drawn from Lucero's study: framing, aligning, paradoxing, abstracting and directing (Lecture 7, PDF p15).

Design workbook: what the design has tried

A design workbook is, in Bill Gaver's sense, a collection of design proposals and other materials drawn together during a project to investigate options for design (Benyon ch8, PDF p218). Its job is to help a design evolve slowly and to keep the lessons from earlier activities. The lecture describes it as a living document of sketches and concepts that records how the design evolved, so that the team can experiment with directions and learn from each one (Lecture 7, PDF p16). The lecture's toolkit describes it as a way to document core ideas and rules (Lecture 7, PDF p13).

Where the slides and book differ. The lecture says a workbook documents core ideas and rules. The book says it investigates options. Both agree that it is the design's own record, so this course uses the book's wording for the purpose.

Produce it • closed book • Tutorial 2 Q5

Close lessons 15 to 15b. Answer Q5 on paper: What is the role of envisionment in UX design, and what techniques are commonly used? Then say how a mood board differs from a design workbook.

reveal model answer

Envisionment is the activity of making design ideas visible, such as sketches, storyboards, mood boards, wireframes and lo-fi or hi-fi prototypes, so that the designer and the people who will use the system can think about the ideas, explore alternatives and evaluate them. It brings abstract ideas to life, exposes flaws and generates new ideas. Commonly used techniques are sketching, storyboarding, mood boards, maps, wireframes and prototypes, including Wizard of Oz prototypes in which a human plays the missing feature, and design workbooks that keep the design's own proposals over a project. A mood board collects images and other stimuli to set how the design should feel, and it is inspiration rather than a formal representation. A design workbook collects the design's own proposals and records what each option taught the team.
