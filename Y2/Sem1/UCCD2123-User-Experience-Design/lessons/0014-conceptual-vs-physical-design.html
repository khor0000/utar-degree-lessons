Conceptual vs physical design

Conceptual design works out what a system must do and what people must know, in the abstract, while physical design works out how that is realised, in the form of sequences of interaction, allocated functions and the look and feel.

Design, one of the four activities from Lesson 11, has two parts. The book puts it simply: "conceptual design is about designing a system in the abstract, physical design is concerned with making things concrete" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79). Conceptual design asks what the system is for. Physical design asks how it will work.

Why this matters. Envisionment (Lesson 15) makes a design visible, and a sketch or prototype already mixes both kinds of design. You need to tell them apart to know which decision you are making.

Looks like "mental model", which appears in the same textbook passage, but it differs in whose picture it is. The conceptual design is the designer's abstract plan of the system. The mental model is the picture a user forms in their head. The book wants the first to lead to the second: the conceptual design should be communicated "so that people will quickly develop a clear mental model" (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p79).

Conceptual is not the same as rough, and physical is not the same as detailed. A conceptual design can be exact: an entity-relationship model is precise. What makes a design conceptual is that it "keep[s] things abstract", focusing "on the what rather than the how", and it avoids assumptions about how functions and information are distributed (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p81). The book also says "there is no clear-cut distinction" between the two, only "degrees of conceptuality" (same page).

One example, four stages

Take a student paying for a canteen meal on the phone. Step through the stages. The first two are conceptual, and the last two are physical.

Conceptual: the things involved. A student, an order, a payment and a receipt. Nothing yet says which screen shows them or where each one sits.

Conceptual: the relations. An order needs a payment, and a payment gives a receipt. This says what the system must know, and it does not yet fix the layout.

Physical: the sequence. The student taps through four steps. The phone shows the QR code, and the counter scanner, a device, confirms it, while the staff member hands over the food. Functions are shared between people and devices.

Physical: the look. Representational design fixes colours, shapes and sizes (Benyon ch3, 3.2, PDF p82). The Pay button is large, cyan and full width, at the bottom of the screen.

Close this lesson, and answer T2 Q4 on paper: How is conceptual design different from physical design in UX?

reveal model answer

Conceptual design is abstract: it concerns the information content and functions a system needs to achieve its purpose, and what someone must know to use it, without deciding how they are distributed. Physical design makes it concrete: it structures interactions into logical sequences, allocates functions and knowledge between people and devices, and fixes the look and feel through colours, shapes and sizes. The two are not sharply separated, and designers move between them, but conceptual design answers what the system is for while physical design answers how it works and looks.
