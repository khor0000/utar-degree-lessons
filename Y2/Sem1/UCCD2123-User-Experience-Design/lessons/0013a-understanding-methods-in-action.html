Understanding in action

Understanding finds out what people need, and designers turn those needs into requirements, which say what the product must do or be like. Each method sees one side of the picture, so the book recommends combining methods rather than choosing one.

Why this matters. Every requirement in the later lessons needs evidence, and that evidence comes from the methods in Lesson 13.

Needs come before requirements

The book does not define "user needs" in one sentence. It describes the focus of understanding as "what people do, or might want to do, how and why they want to do things, and any problems they are having" (Benyon ch7, PDF p175). That is the working definition used here.

User needs: what people do or want to do, why, and where their current way of doing it goes wrong. Benyon ch7, PDF p175 and p178 (working definition, not a book sentence)

A requirement is the next step. The book defines it as "something the product must do or a quality that the product must have" (Benyon ch7, PDF p176). The book contrasts the two: the requirements are the "gain" of the new design, and the problems people have now are the "pain" (Benyon ch7, 7.2, PDF p178).

Getting from one to the other takes a creative leap, which the book calls ideation, the generating of new ideas (PDF p176). Lecture 6 gives the same chain: current activities, user stories, goals and aspirations, then requirements (Lecture 6, PDF p3).

A case: a campus food-ordering app

This case is made up for teaching. It is not from the book. Students at UTAR Kampar are asked how they order lunch, and the team uses five methods, one after another.

Probes. Team does: gives 12 students a camera and a notebook, and asks them to photograph each lunch decision for a week. They get: the timing and reasons, such as the canteen queue being too long after 1.15 pm.

Card sorting. Team does: writes 30 dish names on cards for students to group. They get: groups by speed, sharing and halal, not by stall, which is how the menu is printed.

Working with groups. Team does: runs a focus group with six students and a paper sketch of the order screen. They get: one person often orders for the table, and the group splits the bill on Touch 'n Go.

Fieldwork. Team does: watches the canteen counter through two lunch rushes, without interrupting. They get: orders sent by group chat, and staff calling out names, which no student mentioned in interview.

Artefact collection. Team does: collects paper menus, receipts, parcel slips and order screenshots. They get: prices that differ by stall, and a slip with the whole group's names, which is how staff match orders to people.

Requirement. Team does: writes each finding as a requirement, with its method attached. They get: one group order that one person places and the group splits, with a ready alert. Each requirement keeps its evidence (Lecture 6, PDF p6).

No single method is the best one

The book does not rank these methods. The designer is a bricoleur, a person who improvises with whatever methods suit the setting (Benyon ch7, PDF p207). "Using more than one technique helps to compensate for individual limitations" (PDF p208).

Combining also means checking one method against another. Triangulation means investigating a domain "from at least two complementary positions" (Benyon ch7, PDF p207). In the noticeboard example from Lesson 13, the interview said "I check the noticeboard", and fieldwork showed a group chat instead. Only the two together gave a reliable requirement.

Run it • fresh input • closed book

A campus bookshop wants to know why students buy fewer printed textbooks. Choose two methods from this lesson, say what each gives the team, and name one weakness of each.

reveal model answer

Fieldwork: the team watches students at the shelves, noting what they pick up and put back. It shows what students do and cannot easily say. Weakness: video takes at least three times the raw length to analyse (Benyon ch7, PDF p204).

Interview: the team asks why students buy less and how they study instead. It gives motivations and problems. Weakness: students can only describe what they are aware of (Benyon ch7, PDF p210).
