How wide a journey is, and how many you count

The width of a journey comes from the goal you are designing for. You count one journey per person per goal. Lesson 2 defined a journey as one person and one goal, so this lesson shows how to draw one and how to count them.

You choose how wide a journey is

Narrow. Designing the payment screen? Zoom in on scan and pay.

Medium. Fixing lunch queues? Include choosing the stall and getting the food.

Wide. Asking why students abandon the app? Follow them for months.

The goal decides the zoom. "Pay with the app" is a narrow goal, so its journey starts at the scan. "Decide where to eat and pay" is a wider goal, so its journey starts earlier, when the student is choosing a stall, and includes everything up to the meal.

You count journeys by people and goals

One person with one goal makes one line.

Three products sit on that line, but it is still one journey. Products do not add lines.

A different person or a different goal adds a line. So the stall owner has their own journey.

Working rule (not in the book): one goal gives one journey. Two goals give two journeys, which may share touchpoints.

While paying, the student switches from wi-fi to mobile data.
The night before, the student topped up the e-wallet.
The stall owner checks whether the money arrived.
The scan fails, so the student scans a second time.

A queue is a moment, not a touchpoint

A queue is a moment in the journey, so UX design covers it, because the queue shapes the feeling in Lesson 1. It is not a touchpoint of the designed service, though. A touchpoint is where the person meets the service. The queue is not an interactive system: it does not respond to the person or process content. So there is no interaction for ID to design there.

Group people by what changes their journey

Group people by what changes the journey: their goal, their situation and what they need. A student and a lecturer paying for lunch usually have different situations, so they get separate journeys. Android and iOS users paying for the same lunch share the goal, so they share one journey. Their phones differ, so the touchpoints may need separate versions, but the journey stays one. (This grouping is our working rule. The book's version of it is the persona, which comes later.)
