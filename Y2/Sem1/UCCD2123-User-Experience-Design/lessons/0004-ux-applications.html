Where UX shows up

UX applies wherever people use interactive technology. The slides group its applications into four areas.

Interactive technology is something that can "respond dynamically to people's actions" and process information. A chair reacts physically but processes no information, so it is not interactive technology. A lawn-mowing service done entirely by people is not an interactive system. If you book and pay for it through an app, those touchpoints are, and UX covers them. So UX covers interactive products, services and systems, not every service. The umbrella term for all of them is interactive systems (Benyon ch1, 1.2).

Tutorial Q3 asks for two examples. An example scores better when you name the product and say what its UX does for the person.

The four areas

The grouping is a sorting aid from the slides, not a formal taxonomy from the textbook, so the areas can overlap. An ATM fits "public spaces" as well as "everyday devices". It is listed under everyday devices because most people use it routinely. In an exam, either choice works if you justify it.

Two ready-made examples

The iPhone. Multi-touch replaced physical keys, and the phone senses how it is held and rotates the screen itself (Lecture 1, p7).

The Nest thermostat. A simple interface and phone control make home heating easy to set, and it links to smoke alarms and cameras (Lecture 1, p8).
