Authentication factors: three kinds of proof

Authentication is proving who you are. A factor is one kind of proof, and there are three kinds: something you have, something you know, and something you are.

Why this matters: every login, to a student portal or an e-wallet, asks you to prove you are you. The three kinds tell you what a login is made of. Focus on: the three categories, and counting categories rather than answers. Skip for now: how the system stores your password safely (hashing, lesson 21), and the protocols that use these checks (deck slides 72–77). How you'll be tested: explain in words, no calculator. The old-format October 2025 paper asked "Briefly explain TWO (2) types of authentication factors" for 5 marks. The June 2026 format is not confirmed.

Lesson 8 asked whether a message really came from Ali. This lesson asks whether the person at the keyboard really is Ali.

You might reasonably wonder: does passing this check decide what I may open? No. Authentication only proves who you are. The Unix permissions you met in lesson 4 decide what you may do after you log in.

1. The three factors

The deck says identity is "usually based on the combination of 3 factors" (slide 61). An authentication factor is one of those kinds of proof. It has three parts. What it is: one kind of proof of identity. What value it holds: a thing you carry, a secret you type, or a feature of your body. Why it is needed: the system cannot see who is typing. It can only check what the person presents.

Slide error. Slide 66's title reads "Inheritance Factors", but its body says "Inherent". The correct term is inherence (something you are).

2. Multi-factor means different categories

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) means two or more factors from different categories. An OTP (one-time password) is a code that works once and then expires.

3. Biometrics: what the deck requires

Biometric means a measure of a body or behaviour trait used to identify a person (slide 67). A sensor reads the trait and compares it with stored templates, the saved copies of accepted users' traits. The deck says a good trait is universal (almost everyone has it), distinctive (each person differs noticeably), permanent (it does not change much over time) and collectable (it can be measured effectively) (slide 68).

Not in the deck: slides 63 and 65 give advantages and limitations for ownership and knowledge. For biometrics, slide 70 asks you to score the traits against the four requirements but prints no scores. So this lesson gives no biometric pros or cons.

4. Check yourself

Check 1: sort six items into factors

Put each item under ownership, knowledge or inherence. Try it before you open the reveal.

  1. The PIN you type for your Touch 'n Go eWallet.
  2. A six-digit code texted to your phone for login.
  3. Your student ID card, tapped on a gate reader.
  4. Your fingerprint on a hostel door reader.
  5. The answer to "what was your first pet's name?"
  6. An iris scan at a lab door.
Reveal, step by step
  1. PIN: knowledge. It is typed, and only you should know it.
  2. Texted code: ownership. Your phone is what you have, and the code is an OTP.
  3. Student ID card: ownership. It is a card you hold and present (slide 62 lists RFID cards).
  4. Fingerprint: inherence. It is part of your body (slide 66).
  5. Secret question: knowledge. Slide 64 lists it.
  6. Iris scan: inherence. Slide 66 lists iris scanning.

A Touch 'n Go card with its PIN is multi-factor. Which two categories are these?

Yes. The card is something you have, and the PIN is something you know.
The card is ownership and the PIN is knowledge. Both are different categories, so this is multi-factor.

You log in with a password, then answer your secret question. How many factors is that?

Yes. Both are things you know, so they make one knowledge factor.
Both items are knowledge. Two answers from one category are still one factor.
Paper practice: styled after Oct 2025 Q1(f), 5 marks

Briefly explain TWO types of authentication factors used to verify identity. Give an example of each.

Reveal a model answer

Two different categories, one example each:

  • Ownership (something you have): the system checks that you hold an item or code, such as an OTP sent to your phone or an RFID card.
  • Knowledge (something you know): the system checks a secret you type, such as a password or PIN.

This is a model answer, not the official mark scheme. Two examples from one category would not count as two types.
