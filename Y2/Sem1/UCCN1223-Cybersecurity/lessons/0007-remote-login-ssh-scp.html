Remote login: telnet, ssh and scp

telnet and ssh both give you a shell on another computer, but only ssh encrypts what crosses the network.

Looks like your own shell from lesson 1. The commands are identical; they simply run on the other machine, so read the prompt to know which computer you are on.

Step 1: find the address

An IP address identifies a computer on the network. On the target machine, /sbin/ifconfig prints it after inet addr:, for example 172.16.10.20. On newer systems the label is just inet.

Step 2: compare the two logins

telnetssh
One-second ruleReadable on the wireEncrypted on the wire
Why both matterThe older tool, on your sheet to show the riskThe modern standard
When to useOnly when the lab tells you toEvery real remote login
$ telnet 172.16.20.110
$ ssh -oHostKeyAlgorithms=+ssh-dss username@172.16.20.110
$ logout

In username@172.16.20.110, the @ reads as "this user at this computer". The -oHostKeyAlgorithms=+ssh-dss part lets ssh accept an old server. Treat it as ignore-for-now: copy it exactly and try without it only if told to.

Step 3: download a file (Question 5)

The handout never names a tool for this. scp copies files over ssh, and takes a source, then a destination:

$ scp -oHostKeyAlgorithms=+ssh-dss username@172.16.20.110:/home/student/file.txt .

The : separates the computer from the path on it. The final . means "the folder I am in now". Your instructor gives the real address and name. Ask them if they expect a different method.

Critical Exam Trap • The Final Dot in scp

scp needs a destination, so the last argument cannot be left out.

Correct (earns marks)Incorrect (loses marks)
scp user@ip:/path/file .scp user@ip:/path/file

Why is ssh preferred over telnet?

Yes. Without encryption, anyone listening can read the password.
Think about what crosses the network and who could read it.
