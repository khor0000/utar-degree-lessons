RSA public-key encryption

RSA encrypts with a public pair of numbers, (e, n). Only the private number d can decrypt, because d is the inverse that undoes e.

Why this matters: every cipher so far in this topic needs both people to share one secret key first. RSA removes that need: you publish one key, and only your private key opens what people send you. Focus on: the three walks, in order: make the keys, encrypt, decrypt. Skip for now: why RSA is hard to break, and real key sizes. How you'll be tested: by hand, reducing mod n at every step. Lab 2 Q4 uses the same steps with n = 77. Neither old-format paper asks it.

Lesson 18a gave you the modular inverse: the number that undoes a multiplication mod a number. RSA uses one to make its private key.

Looks like the one shared key of lesson 9, because both are a secret number that scrambles the message. Why it differs: RSA has two keys. The public one can be given to anyone, and only its partner, the private one, opens the message. Two people never need to agree on a secret first.
Looks like the modular inverse of lesson 18a, because d undoes e the way 7 undoes 3 mod 20. Why it differs: d is the inverse mod a helper number z, not mod n. Section 1 builds z.

1. Make the keys

Pick two primes (whole numbers whose only factors are 1 and themselves): p = 3 and q = 11. Each stage is one step. Press Next, or the arrow keys:

p = 3 and q = 11

n = 3 × 11 = 33

z = 2 × 10 = 20

e = 7, and 7 shares no factor with 20

3 × 7 = 21, and 21 mod 20 = 1, so d = 3

Public key (7, 33). Private key d = 3.

z (helper number): (1) what it is: a number worked out from the two primes; (2) what it holds: (p − 1)(q − 1), which is 20 here; (3) why it is needed: d must undo e inside z. You never publish it.
The rule: n = p × q; z = (p − 1)(q − 1); choose e coprime to z; find d, the inverse of e mod z. Public key (e, n), private key d.
Critical Exam Trap • Find d Mod z, Not Mod n

Working d mod n looks just as tidy, and 19 is a true inverse mod 33. But 19 decrypts 20 to 5, not 14.

Correct (earns marks)Incorrect (loses marks)
3 × 7 = 21, and 21 mod 20 = 1, so d = 37 × 19 = 133, and 133 mod 33 = 1, so d = 19

2. Encrypt M = 14 with the public key

Here M is the message as a number, and C is the ciphertext (the encrypted number). The formula is C = Me mod n. The small raised number is the exponent, so 147 means 14 multiplied by itself seven times. Do not write that whole product out. Reduce mod 33 at every step instead.

C = 147 mod 33

142 = 196, and 196 mod 33 = 31

144 = 31 × 31 = 961, and 961 mod 33 = 4

4 × 31 = 124, and 124 mod 33 = 25

25 × 14 = 350, and 350 mod 33 = 20, so C = 20

The rule: encrypt with C = Me mod n, using the public key (e, n). Anyone can do this.

3. Decrypt C = 20 with the private key

Now use M = Cd mod n with d = 3 and n = 33.

M = 203 mod 33

202 = 400, and 400 mod 33 = 4

4 × 20 = 80, and 80 mod 33 = 14, so M = 14

Why does decrypting undo encrypting?

Encrypting and then decrypting raises the message to the power e × d, which is 7 × 3 = 21. Because 21 mod 20 = 1, the power comes back to the start, and the message returns. The full proof is not needed in this course. We use the result.

Check yourself

Check 1: fresh input, p = 5, q = 11, e = 3, M = 4

Make the keys, then encrypt M = 4. Give n, z, d and C. Try it on paper before you open the reveal.

Reveal, step by step
  1. n = 5 × 11 = 55.
  2. z = (5 − 1)(11 − 1) = 4 × 10 = 40.
  3. Find d with (d × 3) mod 40 = 1. Try 27: 3 × 27 = 81, and 81 mod 40 = 1. So d = 27.
  4. Encrypt: C = 43 mod 55. 43 = 64, and 64 mod 55 = 9. So C = 9.

Bob publishes his public key. Alice encrypts a message with it. Who can decrypt it?

Yes. A message encrypted with the public key is opened only with the matching private key.
Decryption needs the private key. Only Bob holds it, so only Bob can read the message.

When you find d, which number is the inverse taken mod?

Yes. d is the inverse of e mod z. Using n gives the wrong d, as the trap box showed.
d is found mod z. Using n gives d = 19, which turns the ciphertext 20 back into 5, not 14.
