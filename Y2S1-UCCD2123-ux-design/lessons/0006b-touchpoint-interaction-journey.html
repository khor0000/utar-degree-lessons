Touchpoint, interaction, journey

A touchpoint is a place or moment where a person meets a service; an interaction is what happens there, the person acts and the system responds. ID works on the interaction at one touchpoint. UX design works on the whole set of touchpoints along the journey.

Benyon calls a touchpoint a "(or service moment)" (ch4). You met the journey in Lesson 2: everything one person goes through to reach one goal. A touchpoint is one stop on that journey.

Benyon says a service has "multiple touchpoints" where people encounter it, and its interactions "happen over time". So touchpoints should "demonstrate a consistent look and feel, and present consistent values".

Where touchpoints sit

One product can hold several touchpoints. An e-wallet app has an onboarding screen, a payment screen, a push notification (a message the app sends to your phone) and a help chat, and each one is its own touchpoint.

Some touchpoints are not products at all. A sign, a cashier and a phone call are touchpoints too, because the person meets the service there.

ID is not split per product. Two products do not mean two ID jobs. ID works wherever an interaction has to be designed: on a screen, at a counter, or on a phone call.

One journey, five touchpoints

DuitNow QR is Malaysia's national QR payment code. At a mamak stall, the customer's e-wallet app scans the code on the stall's QR sticker. Paying this way is one journey with five touchpoints. The diagram draws the line first, then zooms into the app.

Paying at a mamak stall is one journey. Each dot is a place or moment where the customer meets the service. The whole line is UX design, because all five must look and behave consistently.

Zoom into the e-wallet app and its interaction is four steps in a set order. Deciding which steps the person does, which the app does, and their order is the ID job.

What ID means

Benyon defines interaction design as "the allocation of functions to human agency or to technology and with the structuring and sequencing of the interactions" (ch3, 3.2). Plainly: ID decides what the person does, what the phone does, and in what order.

The test: if the fix reorders steps or changes what the system does, it is ID; if it adds or changes a touchpoint, or makes touchpoints match each other, it is UX design.

Run it • fresh input

For Grab food delivery, name three touchpoints, one ID decision at one of them, and one UX design decision across them.

reveal model answer

Three touchpoints: the order screen in the app, the push notification that says the rider is on the way, and the in-app chat with the rider.

ID decision (order screen): the app shows the total before it asks for the payment PIN or biometric, so the customer sees what they are paying first. This reorders steps, so it is ID.

UX design decision (across them): the order screen, the notification and the chat all use the same status words ("on the way", "arrived") and the same tone, so the customer reads one consistent story over time.
